

## Towards Architectural-Level Safety Control: Relocating Hazardous Knowledge into Removable Experts in Mixture-of-Experts Models

[TODO: author list and affiliations]

Abstract | Large language models acquire broad knowledge from web-scale corpora during pretraining, including knowledge that could be hazardous if misused. Removing such knowledge from the model itself without degrading general capability is desirable but difficult, as it is deeply entangled with general capability within the same parameters. Existing safety alignment and unlearning methods typically adjust these shared parameters, tending to suppress the expression of hazardous knowledge rather than remove it. As a result, the knowledge remains in the model and is readily recoverable by subsequent fine-tuning. To address this limitation, we propose REX (Relocating into Removable EXperts), a knowledge relocation method that separates hazardous knowledge from general capability at the architectural level. Exploiting the modular structure of Mixture-of-Experts models, REX expands the model with a set of new, physically removable experts designated to hold hazardous knowledge. Through expert expansion, routing differentiation and selective gradient masking, it relocates hazardous knowledge out of the original experts and concentrates it in the new ones. Excising these experts yields a model suitable for public release. On Intern-S2, Qwen3.5-35B-A3B and Gemma-4-26B-A4B, across biosecurity and cybersecurity, excising the new experts lowers WMDP accuracy by up to 48.39 points while general capability changes by at most 9.48 points, so the full model, with these experts intact, retains the hazardous capability for authorized use. The excised model removes hazardous knowledge on par with the strongest of ten parameter-level unlearning methods, and it is the only one that preserves this removal under subsequent fine-tuning: after 250M tokens of standard fine-tuning on Qwen3.5, Representation Misdirection for Unlearning (RMU) rebounds on WMDP-Bio from 44.07% to 78.32%, whereas REX rises only from 32.44% to 42.81%.

## 1. Introduction

Hazardous knowledge and general knowledge are deeply entangled in pretrained large language models. Pretraining inevitably teaches a model dual-use knowledge, such as synthesis routes in biology or attack techniques in cybersecurity, and this knowledge shares parameters with general knowledge, so the same weights encode legitimate academic competence and potentially dangerous capability. For open-weight models, external safeguards can be removed once the weights are released, so the hazardous knowledge itself should be addressed properly. An ideal removal approach should satisfy the following requirements:

1. Hazardous capability needs to be removed as thoroughly as possible, while doing minimal damage to general capability.

2. Removal should not come at the cost of destruction: developers still need to serve authorized research and defense users with the full capability, while releasing a version with controlled hazardous capability to the public.

3. The released model should remain safe after standard post-training, since open-weight models are routinely fine-tuned after release. Yet when unlearning only adjusts existing parameter values, standard supervised fine-tuning can restore or strengthen the removed knowledge, even on data unrelated to the hazardous domain [Doshi and Stickland, 2024].

Most existing approaches share a common limitation. They modify the original parameter set without changing the model architecture. They therefore fall short of the three requirements above in three related ways. First, behavioral interventions such as refusal training and inference-time filtering leave the hazardous knowledge encoded in the model unchanged and provide only shallow alignment [Qi et al., 2025a]. Unlearning methods such as Representation Misdirection for Unlearning (RMU) [Li et al., 2024], NPO [Zhang et al., 2024] and gradient ascent [Jang et al., 2023] act directly on the original parameters that also support general capability, so removing hazardous capability also affects general capability. Second, none of these approaches provides a single model architecture from which both a full model and a public model with a separately removable hazardous component can be delivered. Third, because current alignment and unlearning methods suppress or overwrite hazardous knowledge rather than removing it from the model, later post-training can make the same knowledge visible again (even when the new data is unrelated to the hazardous domain [Doshi and Stickland, 2024]). The released model therefore cannot be expected to remain safe after post-training. These limitations motivate a change at the architectural level. If we can augment a pretrained model with additional parameters, concentrate hazardous knowledge in them while preserving general capability in the original parameters, and remove these additional parameters before release, this could satisfy the three requirements above.

To realize this idea, we turn to Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) models [Shazeer et al., 2017; Fedus et al., 2022] and propose REX (Relocating into Removable EXperts), a method for relocating hazardous knowledge into newly added experts that can be removed before public release. MoE models are well suited to this approach because their feed-forward parameters are partitioned into independently addressable experts, the expert set can be expanded, a trainable router determines which experts process each token, and selected experts can be removed at delivery. REX expands a pretrained MoE model with dedicated target experts and relocates hazardous knowledge from the original experts into them, retaining general capability in the original experts and thereby separating hazardous capability from general capability at the architectural level.

The main challenge is to achieve this capability separation when hazardous and general knowledge are already mixed across the original experts. REX addresses this challenge through three components: expert expansion, selective gradient masking and routing differentiation. Expert expansion initializes

the target experts from the original experts that hazardous data relies on beyond general data, so relocation starts from experts already aligned with the target domain. Selective gradient masking lets target data update only the target experts, and on general data it excludes the target experts before top-K selection, so the original experts learn to maintain general capability without them while the hazardous knowledge they hold receives no training signal and fades. Routing differentiation directs target-related tokens toward the target experts and keeps general tokens away from them. After the target experts are excised, a light RMU cleanup removes the small residue of hazardous knowledge left in the retained parameters. Through these three components, REX separates hazardous capability from general capability at the architectural level. This separation allows general capability to improve during post-training without a substantial rebound in hazardous capability. Retaining or removing the target experts yields a full model for authorized users or a controlled model for public release.

An intuitive comparison is shown in Figure 1. The pretrained model can be viewed as a glass of water containing general knowledge (green) and hazardous knowledge (red) mixed together. Conventional alignment and unlearning operate directly inside this glass, trying to make the water appear green by suppressing hazardous outputs, while the underlying mixture remains unchanged. Our method uses the routing mechanism of an MoE model as an additional layer of separation. In this analogy, the router acts like a filter that directs the (red) hazardous signal to newly added target experts, while the (green) general signal remains in the original experts. As training continues on both data sources, routing and parameter updates reinforce this division. The model can then be separated into a full form that retains hazardous capability and an excised form with the target experts removed, providing a safer and more controllable model for release.

Our contributions are threefold.

• We propose REX, the first method to separate and remove hazardous capability in MoE models at the architectural level. REX moves the hazardous knowledge already present in a pretrained MoE model into expanded target experts while keeping general capability in the original experts. We measure this separation by comparing the full model with the excised model, obtained by removing the expanded experts, on hazardous and general benchmarks. In both settings and on three open-source MoE models, the excised model scores substantially lower on hazardous capability while its general capability remains largely unchanged (Table 1).

• Although REX is designed for knowledge removal and capability separation rather than for benchmark forgetting alone, when applied to the base model it removes hazardous knowledge on the standard WMDP-Bio and WMDP-Cyber unlearning benchmarks on par with the strongest of 10 parameter-level unlearning baselines (Sections 4.3 and 4.5).

• We show that REX is the only method that preserves forgetting under post-training. In contrast to the baselines that reach a low starting point and then climb back towards the original model under the same standard fine-tuning, the delivered model of REX remains at a low level of hazardous knowledge. On Qwen3.5, RMU rises from 44.07% to 78.32% on WMDP-Bio, close to the 83.19% of the original model, whereas REX stays at 42.81% and rises by only 10.45 points over the same trajectory (Section 4.4).

## 2. Related Work

Machine unlearning removes hazardous knowledge by changing parameter values in place, including in recent methods designed for Mixture-of-Experts models, and a growing body of work shows that such removal does not survive later training. RMU [Li et al., 2024] perturbs intermediate representations of forget data toward a random direction while preserving those of retain data, and it was introduced together with the WMDP benchmark that we use to measure hazardous knowledge.

Gradient ascent [Jang et al., 2023] maximizes the loss on forget data, gradient difference [Maini et al., 2024] adds a retain term to it, and NPO [Zhang et al., 2024] replaces its unbounded objective with a preference-style loss that avoids collapse. All of these methods act on parameters that general capability also uses. As a result, Doshi and Stickland [2024] show that ordinary fine-tuning on unrelated data restores the knowledge they remove, and Qi et al. [2025b] find that safeguards on open-weight models are routinely undone by later training. Tamper-resistant methods try to make the removal harder to reverse by shaping the loss landscape around the released weights, through adversarial meta-learning against simulated fine-tuning attacks in TAR [Tamirisa et al., 2025], an invariance objective in ILU [Wang et al., 2025a], or sharpness-aware minimization [Fan et al., 2025a], yet the suppressed knowledge still resides in parameters that remain in the model. For MoE models, recent work uses the expert structure to make unlearning more targeted. SEUF [Zhuang et al., 2024] attributes the target knowledge to the experts most engaged with it, confines unlearning to those experts and anchors the router so that they stay selected, and TRACE [Xie et al., 2026] calibrates expert updates in a routing-aware manner. These methods use experts to decide where to unlearn, but they still overwrite parameter values inside experts that the delivered model keeps.

Knowledge localization takes a different approach and confines a capability to designated parameters so that it can be removed as a whole. SGTM [Shilov et al., 2025] applies selective gradient masking during pretraining so that target data updates only a designated subset of parameters, which are then removed, and GRAM [Roland et al., 2026] uses a modular architecture to enable access control over capabilities learned during pretraining. Because both prevent knowledge from entering the rest of the model, they require training from scratch and do not apply to a pretrained model in which the knowledge is already spread across the parameters.

## 3. Method

REX starts from a pretrained MoE model, relocates the hazardous knowledge spread across its original experts into a set of new, physically removable experts, which we call target experts, and builds on this a complete pipeline from base-model delivery to post-trained delivery. Three components perform the relocation, namely expert expansion (Section 3.2), selective gradient masking (Section 3.3) and routing differentiation (Section 3.4). They turn the catastrophic forgetting of continued training into directed forgetting. The original experts are updated only on general data and without the target experts, so the hazardous knowledge they hold receives no training signal and fades, while target data updates only the target experts. Together the three components realize knowledge relocation and capability separation. Excision followed by residual cleanup (Section 3.5) turns separation into more thorough removal from the base model. Post-training on the full structure with excision at delivery (Section 3.6) bounds the growth of hazardous knowledge during post-training. We first fix the notation (Section 3.1).

## 3.1. Preliminaries and notation

We use two kinds of training data. Target data $D _ {harm}$ carries the target knowledge, such as a biosecurity or cybersecurity corpus, and general data $D _ {std}$ covers general capability. In an MoE layer, the router maps the hidden state x of a token to expert logits and routing probabilities

$$
r(x)=W_{r}x,\qquad P(x)=\text{softmax}\big(r(x)\big).\qquad\qquad \qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad(1)
$$

The layer output is the weighted sum of the top-K experts,

$$
y=\sum_{i\in\mathrm{TopK}(r(x))}g_{i}(x)\,E_{i}(x),\qquad\qquad\qquad \qquad(2)
$$

![](https://sce-test.oss-cn-shanghai.aliyuncs.com/261015191820695_1474_e6284d15686544269d174e309d3c07e4/imgs/image4-2.jpg)

where $\operatorname{TopK}(r(x))$ is the set of the K experts with the largest logits, $E _ {i}$ is the i-th expert and $g _ {i}$ are the normalized gate weights. We write $\theta _ {harm}$ for the target experts and their router rows, $\theta _ {std}$ for the original experts and their router rows, and $\theta _ {shared}$ for the remaining parameters, including attention, embeddings, norms, the language-model head and any router parameters not tied to a single expert. Shared-expert MLPs belong to neither group of experts.

## 3.2. Expert expansion

Expert expansion adds the target experts and initializes them by differential-activation expansion. On the training corpora $D _ {std}$ and $D _ {harm}$, we count how often each expert i is selected into the top-K, which gives the frequencies $f _ {i}^{std}$ and $f _ {i}^{harm}$, and compute $\Delta _ {i}=f _ {i}^{harm}-f _ {i}^{std}$. In each layer, the experts with the largest $\Delta _ {i}$ form the source set S. Selecting by the frequency on target data alone tends to pick general-purpose experts that are active on all data, and their copies would carry nothing specific to the target domain. Subtracting the frequency on general data keeps the experts that target data relies on beyond general data, so the target experts match the computation of the target domain from the start. The selection uses only routing statistics on the training data and never reads evaluation data.

For each expert in S, we copy its parameters and router row as a new target expert. We write H for the set of new target experts. All original experts are kept, and the source experts are scaled by a fixed factor s to weaken the hazardous knowledge they hold before training. If the source experts were left unchanged, they would compete with the target experts for target tokens, which makes routing differentiation harder and greatly increases the training budget that relocation needs. If they were set to zero, general capability would suffer severe damage. Scaling weakens the hazardous knowledge in the source experts while keeping most of their general computation.

## 3.3. Selective gradient masking

Selective gradient masking decides where the updates of each data source are allowed to be written. During relocation the shared parameters stay frozen, including attention, embeddings, norms, the language-model head and the shared-expert MLPs, and only the routed experts and the routers are

trained. For the expert parameters, gradients from target tokens update only the target experts, and gradients from general tokens update only the original experts. The routers are updated by both. In terms of parameter sets,

$$
\text{updates}=\begin{cases}\theta_{\text{harm}}\cup\theta_{\text{router}}& \text{on }\mathcal{D}_{\text{harm}},\\ \theta_{\text{std}}\cup\theta_{\text{router}}&\text{on }\mathcal{D}_{\text{std}}, \end{cases}\qquad\qquad(3)
$$

where $\theta _ {router}$ collects all router rows. Forward computation is masked on general data only. A pre-top-K activation mask sets the logits of the target experts to $-\infty$ before the selection,

$$
\tilde{r}_{i}(x)=\begin{cases}r_{i}(x)&i\notin H,\\ -\infty&i\in H,\end{cases}\qquad x\in\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{std}},\qquad\qquad \qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad(4)
$$

so the top-K is taken over the original experts and the gate weights are renormalized. Each token of general data is therefore dispatched without the target experts, and the model must perform well with the original experts alone.

The two rules cover different failure modes, and knowledge cannot be moved out if either one is missing. The gradient mask on target data makes the target experts the only place where new hazardous knowledge is written, but on its own it cannot remove what the original experts already hold, which is why it suffices when pretraining from scratch but not for a pretrained model. The activation mask on general data supplies the other half. The original experts are updated only on general data and always with the target experts absent, so the hazardous knowledge they hold is neither trained further nor relieved by target experts that could take over general computation, and it decays during relocation. Together the two rules give directed forgetting while general capability is trained not to depend on the target experts.

## 3.4. Routing differentiation

Routing differentiation steers target tokens toward the target experts, and together with selective gradient masking it accelerates capability separation. It adds two losses, the mass loss and the rank loss.

The mass loss decides how much routing mass the target experts receive, and the rank loss decides where they sit in the top-K order. The mass loss constrains how much routing mass the target experts receive. For token t in a given layer, let $z _ {t} = \sum _ {i \in H \cap \operatorname{TopK}(r(x _ {t}))} g _ {i}(x _ {t})$ be the normalized top-K gate weight assigned to the target experts. $z _ {t}$ is computed before masking, while the actual dispatch uses the top-K selected again after masking. Writing $\bar{z}$ for the mean of $z _ {t}$ over the tokens of a batch and over MoE layers, the mass loss requires $\bar{z}$ to be no lower than a floor $\tau _ {h}$ on target data and no higher than a ceiling $\tau _ {s}$ on general data,

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\text{mass}}=\begin{cases}\lambda_{h}\max\left(0,\ \tau_{h}-\bar {z}\right)&\text{on }\mathscr{D}_{\text{ harm}},\\ \lambda_{s}\max\left(0,\ \bar{z}-\tau_{s}\right)&\text{ on }\mathscr{D}_{\text{ std} }.\end{cases}\qquad\qquad (5)
$$

so it raises the mass on target tokens and holds it down on general tokens. The hinge form produces gradients only when routing violates this split.

The rank loss constrains the order of the top-K selection instead of the mass. A bound on the mass does not decide which experts are activated, because a target expert enters the activated set as soon as its logit crosses the top-K boundary, even when it then receives little mass. On $D _ {std}$ we therefore write $h _ {\operatorname*{max}} = \operatorname*{max} _ {i \in H} r _ i(x _ t)$ for the largest target-expert logit and $s _ K$ for the K-th largest logit among the original experts, which is the top-K boundary, and require $h _ {max}$ to stay below $s _ K$ by a margin $\delta$.

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\text{rank}}=\mathbb{E}_{t\in\mathcal{D}_{\text{std}}}\left[ \max\left(0,\,\delta-(s_{K}-h_{\text{max}})\right)\right].\qquad\qquad\qquad \qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad(6)
$$

so the target experts stay outside the activated set on general tokens.

The two losses complement each other. The mass loss shapes how much routing mass the target experts receive, but it bounds only the total mass, so a satisfied bound does not prevent an expert from being wrongly selected. The rank loss acts on the top-K selection itself and keeps the target experts out of the activated set on general tokens. Because it constrains the selection so directly, however, its gradient can pull against the language-modeling loss. We therefore use the two losses together, with the rank loss correcting the selection and the mass loss shaping routing more gently. Thus, the final relocation objective is

$$
\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{LM}}+\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{mass}}+ \lambda_{r}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{rank}},\hskip 5cm(7)
$$

where the language-modeling loss is computed and back-propagated under the masking rules of Section 3.3.

## 3.5. Excision and residual cleanup

After relocation we physically remove the target experts and their router rows, then use a light RMU cleanup to clean the small residue that structural removal leaves, obtaining a base model with more thorough removal than direct parameter-level unlearning. Excision deletes the parameters of every expert in H and the corresponding rows of the router, which from then on computes probabilities and top-K selection over the original experts only. The resulting model $A _ {1}$ has the same architecture and parameter layout as B and contains no target experts. Directed forgetting during relocation is not complete: the original experts may keep a small amount of hazardous knowledge, and related representations that pretraining encoded in the frozen shared experts (or dense MLP) are untouched, so this part cannot be removed by deleting target experts.

Residual cleanup faces a much smaller task, so compared with parameter-level unlearning applied to the original model, it can remove more thoroughly at comparable general capability. We apply RMU [Li et al., 2024] to $A _ {1}$. At a chosen monitor layer, it pushes the hidden states of target data toward a fixed random direction and keeps the hidden states of general data close to those of $A _ {1}$ before cleanup. The trainable parameters are limited to the routed-expert MLPs and, when configured for RMU cleanup, the shared-expert MLPs of selected layers; routers, attention, embeddings, norms and the language-model head remain frozen, which gives the base model $A _ {2}$. Shared-expert MLPs are cleanup parameters here, not members of either the harmful or standard expert bank. For Gemma 4 / Cyber, cleanup updates only the down projections of the selected routed experts; the always-on dense MLP remains frozen. As an ablation, matched parameter-level unlearning R applies exactly the same RMU operator, optimizer hyperparameters and forget budget directly to B, with B itself as the reference, so that the only difference between the two is the starting point. For $A _ {2}$, the bulk of the hazardous knowledge was moved into the target experts during relocation and left with excision, so RMU only has to handle the residue; for R, RMU must fight all of the hazardous knowledge inside the shared parameters on its own. Structural removal and parameter cleanup each have their own role in the pipeline: the former removes, as a whole, the bulk of the knowledge concentrated in the target experts, and the latter handles the residue outside that structure.

## 3.6. Post-training and delivery

The key design of the pipeline is to run standard post-training on the recombined full structure and defer excision to delivery, so that the growth of hazardous knowledge during post-training is directed into the target experts and leaves with them. We recombine the base $A _ {2}$ with the target experts of $F _ {1}$ and their router rows, $H _ {1}$, into the full structure $F _ {2}$: all parameters of $A _ {2}$ are kept as they are,

and the target experts and their router rows come from the same-origin $F _ {1}$. Ordinary full-parameter fine-tuning $S _ {std}$ on $F _ {2}$ gives the full instruction model $F _ {3}$, and deleting the target experts of $F _ {3}$ at delivery gives the public instruction model $A _ {3}$. Fine-tuning needs no masks and no additional losses, so the developer can reuse an existing post-training pipeline unchanged.

The growth of hazardous knowledge during post-training is bounded because fine-tuning runs on the full structure, and gradients related to target knowledge flow along the routing path built during relocation into the target experts, which are still present. After routing differentiation, the router already sends target-related tokens to the target experts, so the updates those tokens produce during fine-tuning are written mainly into the target experts and leave with them at delivery, while growth in general capability is written mainly into the retained parameters and stays with the delivered model. Capability growth and risk growth are thereby placed in two different parts of the structure. By contrast, if the same fine-tuning is applied directly to $A _ {2}$ (denoted $A _ {3}$-direct), the model has no experts left to receive the target-related gradients, and this growth can only be written into the retained parameters, with no structural bound. Recombination also lets fine-tuning run on the model's full capacity, so training for instruction following is not affected by excision. The developer thus obtains a paired set of controllable deliverables from one post-training run: $F _ {3}$ for authorized access and $A _ {3}$ for public release. Which artifact is exposed is a release decision, and this extends the capability separation of Section 3.4 to the post-training stage.

This defines our threat model. The releasing party always holds the target experts, and the public model does not contain these parameters. Our claims cover two levels, the unlearned base model and the model after standard post-training, and do not extend to malicious fine-tuning after release. In this setting, the absence of hazardous knowledge from the public model does not rest on a fragile balance among parameter values; it is guaranteed by the computing units that carry that knowledge not being part of the delivered model.

The drop in target knowledge comes from the target experts, not from excising any parameters of the same size (Table 11). On the Intern-S2 / Bio model $F _ {1}$, we remove the same number of original experts as there are target experts in every layer, keep the added experts, and repeat with five random seeds. What takes it away is the set of experts that REX selected and relocated.

The routing diagnostic shows that relocation turns the target experts into the inference path of the target knowledge (Figure 3). On target data $D _ {harm}$ and general data $D _ {std}$, we passively measure the normalized top-K routing mass that each token assigns to the target experts, using the same samples for $F _ {0}$ and $F _ {1}$. After relocation the mass on target tokens rises in all six settings, to 65–76% on Bio and 35–47% on Cyber. On Bio the target experts become the majority path for target tokens, holding over half of the selected mass at most layers of all three backbones. ## 4.5. Ablations

The separation of Section 4.2 requires both mechanisms that REX adds to selective gradient masking (Table 4). The ablation varies the relocation mechanisms on Intern-S2 / Bio from one starting model under the same token budget and data order, and measures separation as the WMDP accuracy that excision takes away from the relocated model, $F _ {1}-A _ {1}$, before residual cleanup. Gradient masking alone, which is the SGTM recipe [Shilov et al., 2025] carried over to a pretrained model, takes away 15.79 points: it keeps target data out of the original experts but leaves the target knowledge they already hold in place. ## 5. Discussion

Moving hazardous knowledge out of the parameters that carry it, rather than suppressing it where it sits, is what turns removal into a structural operation with two deliverable forms. Selective gradient masking alone suffices when the knowledge has not yet entered the model, but in a pretrained model the original experts already hold it. Masking the target experts on general data makes catastrophic forgetting a controllable tool: nothing maintains the target knowledge in the original experts, so they let it go, while the target experts absorb it. Because the knowledge is sealed away rather than erased, one training run yields both a full model for authorized research and defense users and a public model. Adding the target experts back to shared parameters that have already been cleaned raises WMDP-Bio on Qwen3.5 from 32.44% to 80.83%, and after post-training the full model for authorized users $F _ {3}$ stays within 2.27–5.99 points of the original model fine-tuned with the same procedure in all six settings. Parameter-level unlearning leaves a single model, so a developer serving both audiences has to maintain two separate pipelines; REX produces both forms from one lineage and one fine-tuning run.

Structural removal reaches further than parameter-level unlearning because it changes what the cleanup step has to do. Parameter-level unlearning applied to the original model must suppress hazardous knowledge inside parameters that also carry general capability, and every additional point of forgetting is paid for in the shared representation. In REX, excision removes the bulk of the knowledge together with the experts that hold it, taking Intern-S2 from 79.42% to 64.34% on WMDP-Bio before any cleanup. The same RMU operator then only has to handle the residue, and with an identical budget it reaches 29.62% instead of the 33.31% it reaches from B, a gap of 3.69–14.85 points on Bio and 1.46–4.68 points on Cyber. Structure and parameter cleanup are thus complementary rather than competing: structure removes what is concentrated, and cleanup handles what is not.

The bound on post-training growth follows from where fine-tuning gradients go. Fine-tuning on general instruction data also produces gradients that increase hazardous knowledge, and in every route those gradients have to land somewhere. On the parameter-level unlearning routes and on $A _ {3}$-direct, retained parameters are the only place they can go, so the knowledge returns, and the routes that matched $A _ {2}$ before post-training climb back toward the fine-tuned original model. On the recombined full structure, the router sends target-related tokens to the target experts, the corresponding growth lands there, and it leaves at excision. After 250M tokens the full model $F _ {3}$ is back at 78.63% while the delivered $A _ {3}$ stays at 54.99%: the growth is carried by the experts that leave at delivery. REX keeps at least 52% of its base-model removal on Bio and 78% on Cyber, whereas the two strongest baselines of each setting keep at most 37% and 63%. The direct-SFT control locates the bound in the recombination rather than in the low starting point of $A _ {2}$: starting from the same $A _ {2}$ without recombining ends 9.58–20.19 points higher. Whatever the developer later trains on, the knowledge that carries risk is stored in parameters that are not in the delivered model, so capability growth and risk growth move independently.

The same design carries over across MoE architectures because it relies only on experts and a trainable router. Intern-S2 and Qwen3.5 use shared experts next to routed experts, while Gemma 4 uses an always-on dense MLP and per-expert router scales. In each case the non-routed components belong to the frozen shared parameters, and the per-expert scales are split with their experts, so the expansion initialization and the routing losses need no architecture-specific changes. The same ordering after fine-tuning, with REX lowest among usable methods, holds across the three backbones and both domains. General capability is preserved along with it. Standard post-training raises the MMLU of the delivered model by 1.49–1.80 points on Bio, and what the public model does not recover is confined to the subjects adjacent to the target domain. On Intern-S2, $A _ {3}$ scores 83.55% on all subjects outside

the target domain against 83.97% for the original model under the same $S _ {std}$. The remaining gap therefore sits where the target knowledge itself sits, which is where a removal method is meant to act.

## 6. Conclusion

We have shown that relocating hazardous knowledge into removable experts turns its governance in MoE models from in-place erasure into a structural operation, with three consequences. First, on the base model, excision followed by a light residual cleanup reaches the removal level of the strongest parameter-level unlearning methods. Second, REX separates capability rather than destroying it: through expert expansion, selective gradient masking and routing differentiation, one model has a full form that keeps the capability for authorized use and an excised form for public release. Third, post-training on the full structure and excising at delivery bounds the growth of hazardous knowledge under standard fine-tuning across every tested budget, while the delivered model keeps improving on general capability and what it does not recover is confined to the subjects adjacent to the target domain, so that capability growth is decoupled from risk growth.

## A. Experimental setup

Table 6 lists the recipe of every setting. All settings share the expansion of M = 8 target experts per MoE layer, chosen by differential-activation expansion, with Gaussian noise of scale $10^{-5}$ added to the cloned experts, and relocation uses cosine decay with $\theta _ {shared}$ frozen. The Cyber forget corpus is much smaller than the Bio corpus, so the Cyber relocation budgets are correspondingly smaller. Residual cleanup monitors one early layer and updates the routed- and shared-expert MLPs of the three layers up to it, while routers, attention, embeddings, norms and the language-model head remain frozen.

The excised side of REX is evaluated during fine-tuning by masking the target experts before top-K selection, because the trajectory evaluation runs on the resident training model. Table 7 compares this mode with physical excision on the same delivered base, which was evaluated both ways, and the two agree to within one standard error on WMDP and MMLU. The IFEval level differs across backbones under the shared $S _ {std}$ for all routes alike, so it is compared within a backbone rather than across backbones.

Our TRACE comparison uses the implementation associated with version 1 of Xie et al. [2026], together with a hyperparameter search that follows the paper. The authors withdrew the paper on 23 September 2026 and identified an error in the per-expert gradient decomposition of Eqs. (4)–(6), which we do not use. The comparison therefore rests on the released implementation, and we report the measured results as an empirical baseline only; the withdrawal notice is available on the arXiv record.
